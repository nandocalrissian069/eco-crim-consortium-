A holistic network for environmental criminal accountability

A holistic network of civil society, NGOs & academia — working on the investigation and accountability of large-scale environmental crimes across international, regional & national systems.

Civil society organisations, NGOs, and academic institutions already hold the investigative expertise. The Consortium gives that expertise structure through three organising ideas — complementarity, plurality, and a multi-level approach — codifying it into shared protocols useable across the full range of enforcement and crime-control systems in which environmental accountability is pursued. We are institutionally independent of any court or prosecutorial authority.

§ 01 — The proposition

A holistic system, not a new institution

The actors who already investigate large-scale environmental harm hold the expertise. The Consortium gives shape to a holistic system across the enforcement actors with whom they already engage.

Civil society, NGOs, and academia present themselves and their work. Complementarity, plurality, and the multi-level approach sit at the heart.

The Consortium does not absorb its members into any court or prosecutorial authority.
It does not claim privileged status with any institution.
It coordinates; it does not represent or speak on members' behalf.

Civil society organisations, NGOs, and academic institutions already build investigative archives, conduct field, scientific, and forensic work, develop methodology, and pursue accountability across multiple legal and political fora — on their own mandates and with their own resources.

The ICC Office of the Prosecutor, through the December 2025 Policy on Addressing Environmental Damage Through the Rome Statute, has committed to cooperation with the very community whose expertise this work depends on, and to an interdisciplinary approach drawing on law, science, technology, public health, economics, and Indigenous knowledge. That Policy is one of several significant openings across the enforcement landscape toward which the Consortium orients itself.

The Consortium gives that meeting structure: a place for the network to present itself, a forum for its interaction, and an explicit orientation toward three organising ideas — complementarity in how the network engages every actor, plurality in what it produces and who produces it, and the multi-level approach as the architecture across which both operate.

§ 03 — How we interact

Three communities, one holistic system

The network of civil society, NGOs, and academia holds the investigative expertise. Each community plays a distinct role. Together they generate the added value that no single actor could produce on its own.

Civil society

Frontline documentation & testimony

Communities, frontline investigators, and Indigenous knowledge holders document ecosystem-level destruction as it occurs, preserve testimony, and build the archival record on which any later legal analysis depends.

ROLE — presence, archive, witness
NGOs

Forensic & analytical capacity

Specialist organisations bring forensic sampling, remote-sensing and hyperspectral analysis, bioacoustic and AI-assisted methods, chain-of-custody discipline, and the situational reach to investigate where institutions cannot.

ROLE — investigation, verification, methodology
Academia

Doctrine, method & codification

Universities and research centres develop the doctrinal scholarship, codify the network's methods into the family of protocols, and host the structured reflection that turns practice into shared standards.

ROLE — doctrine, codification, reflection
The network engages with the full range of enforcement and crime-control actors — national prosecutors, specialised environmental agencies, regional cooperation bodies, and international mechanisms. The ICC OTP's December 2025 Policy is a significant and vital opening within that broader picture, taken seriously as one important node — complementary to, not substitutive for, the many other channels through which the network engages.
§ 04 — The organising ideas

Complementarity, plurality, multi-level

Three concepts shape what the Consortium is and what it adds. They are not technical doctrines borrowed from any single institution. They are the general organising ideas of the network — the frame within which civil society, NGO, and academic expertise combines across geographies, methods, and layers of enforcement to produce something that no actor could produce alone.

The three ideas are mutually reinforcing. Complementarity describes the stance the Consortium takes toward every actor and every institution with which its members engage. Plurality describes the shape of what the Consortium produces and the community that produces it. The multi-level approach describes the architecture across which both of these operate. Taken together, they explain how added value emerges from working together — rather than from any single organisation, forum, or jurisdiction working on its own.

Idea 01

Complementarity

The Consortium is complementary by design. It does not substitute for any member organisation, any national authority, any regional body, or any international mechanism. It adds to what each already does, filling the connective and methodological gaps that no single actor can close on its own. The protocols complement existing investigative practice; the training complements existing institutional capacity; the case-analysis forum complements the independent judgment of each member and each enforcement actor. The principle expressed formally in Article 17 of the Rome Statute — that national authorities bear primary responsibility, with international mechanisms reserved for last-resort situations — is one specific legal instantiation of this broader idea, not its source.

Idea 02

Plurality

The Consortium is plural by design. Its membership spans civil society, NGOs, and academic institutions, each with its own mandate, methodology, and voice. Its protocols are deliberately plural — a family of standards rather than a single fixed instrument — because the realities of environmental evidence differ across modalities, situations, and the legal questions they are mobilised to answer. Its case-analysis sessions bring together legal, scientific, and field perspectives that would otherwise speak past each other. Plurality is not a soft commitment to diversity; it is a methodological choice, the only choice that fits the empirical complexity of large-scale environmental harm.

Idea 03

Multi-level

The Consortium is multi-level by design. Environmental enforcement runs across national, regional, and international layers simultaneously, and the Consortium's methods are configured to operate across all three at once rather than privileging any one. The majority of large-scale environmental cases will be investigated and prosecuted at the national level, under domestic criminal law. A growing body of regional enforcement — the 2024 EU Environmental Crime Directive, regional human rights systems, and specialised cooperation bodies, including those within the African Union — sits above and alongside those national systems. International mechanisms occupy a narrower space, reserved for situations where domestic and regional accountability is unavailable or has demonstrably failed.

The added value of the Consortium is what these three ideas produce together. Complementarity on its own would be a stance without method. Plurality on its own would be a community without coherence. The multi-level approach on its own would be an architecture without voice. Combined, they turn a distributed community of investigators, scientists, and scholars into a methodologically disciplined network capable of engaging the full enforcement landscape — contributing to each layer on its own terms, without substituting for any of them.

Within international criminal practice, the formal doctrine of complementarity is expressed in Article 17 of the Rome Statute, which reserves the primary responsibility for investigating and prosecuting Rome Statute crimes to national jurisdictions. The Consortium's broader idea of complementarity is consistent with that formal doctrine, and informs how members approach international engagement when it becomes appropriate — but it is not derived from it. The idea precedes the doctrine, and reaches well beyond it.
§ 05 — A policy opening

The ICC OTP's December 2025 Policy — a vital opening

The three organising ideas find one of their most significant recent openings in the ICC Office of the Prosecutor's December 2025 Policy, which exemplifies both the multi-level approach the Consortium takes and the complementary stance in which it engages external actors.

Launched on 4 December 2025, the Policy on Addressing Environmental Damage Through the Rome Statute explains how existing core crimes may be investigated and charged when committed by means of, or resulting in, environmental damage.

The Policy covers all four core crimes — genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and aggression — when they are committed through or result in environmental harm. It adopts an explicitly interdisciplinary approach, drawing on law, science, technology, public health, economics, and Indigenous knowledge. And it expressly commits the Office to cooperation with civil society organisations and other non-State actors whose expertise or information can support accountability.

The Consortium takes that opening seriously as one important node in its work. The Policy's doctrinal and methodological commitments align closely with the way the network already investigates and analyses environmental harm, and its explicit opening to civil society and academic engagement is substantive. We engage with that opening deliberately, methodically, and on the same terms applicable to all external actors — without any claim to privileged status.

The Consortium is institutionally separate from any court or prosecutorial authority, including the ICC. It engages with the Office, and with every other enforcement actor at every level, on terms that preserve its own independence and that of its member organisations.
§ 06 — Pilots

Two pilot case studies

The Consortium's method is being tested, in operational conditions, through two pilot case studies chosen for their contrast. The pilots will generate the first iterations of our protocols and the first published analytical products — and they are where the three organising ideas meet the ground.

Pilot case study 01

Ukraine

Armed conflict, environmental harm, and multi-layer accountability.

The Consortium's first operational pilot. Network members hold deep existing documentation of the environmental dimensions of the conflict; the pilot is testing how the Consortium's protocols and case-analysis forum perform under real-time conditions, and across all three enforcement layers — supporting Ukrainian domestic prosecutions, engaging regional mechanisms including the EU, and holding open the question of international engagement for situations where domestic accountability does not reach.

  • Armed conflict
  • Environmental harm
  • Multi-layer accountability
  • Network coordination
Read the Ukraine pilot → Pilot case study 02

Democratic Republic of Congo

Extractive industries, transboundary harm, and community-centred accountability.

The second pilot is deliberately chosen for contrast. A context of long-standing extractive-industry harm, cross-border ecological consequences, Indigenous and affected-community engagement at the centre, and a different mix of national, regional (African Union, African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights), and international enforcement pathways. The DRC pilot is designed to demonstrate that the Consortium's method is not a Europe-only or armed-conflict-only toolkit, but a method that holds across geographies and contexts. It is anchored in a Congo-focused subconsortium already active on the ground, working in close coordination with the broader network and on an equal footing with its other members.

  • Extractive industries
  • Transboundary harm
  • Indigenous rights
  • Regional & national accountability
Read the DRC pilot →

The pilots have a visible governance anchor: each is selected by the Steering Committee on the recommendation of the Scientific and Technical Advisory Group and the Civil Society Council, and each has a convening home among the member organisations working with a Consortium-side coordinator. Further pilots will be considered as the network matures.

§ 07 — Method

A plurality of protocols

The Consortium codifies the investigative expertise its members already hold into a family of protocols — usable at the national, regional, and international level alike.

Documentation of ecosystem-level harm Scientific evidence integration Chain of custody & authentication Ethical engagement of communities Procedural pathways at every level Usable at national, regional & international levels

The protocols are intentionally plural. The empirical and procedural realities of environmental evidence differ across modalities, situations, and the legal questions they are mobilised to answer — a single instrument cannot govern them all.

They are not invented from scratch. They are assembled, harmonised, and published as a coherent family from the practice that already exists across the network, and refined iteratively through use. A single protocol in the family may travel across all three enforcement layers; the method is designed with that reach in mind from the start.

§ 08 — Workstreams

Four interlocking workstreams

Designed to be mutually reinforcing rather than siloed — each anchored in the three organising ideas and alive to the openings created across the enforcement landscape.

W—01 Protocols & Investigative Standards Codifies the investigative expertise the network already holds into the family of protocols, the cross-disciplinary glossaries, and the analytical templates on which the Consortium's method rests. The first protocol drafts are being tested through the Ukraine and DRC pilots and will be published as a coherent family as they stabilise.
W—02 Doctrinal Development Peer-reviewed scholarship on causation in complex ecosystems, Article 8(2)(b)(iv), the doctrinal positioning of ecocide, complementarity in environmental cases, and reparations regimes for ecosystem-level injuries. Doctrinal outputs track both pilots, with particular attention to how complementarity, modes of liability, and reparations design play out across armed-conflict and extractive-harm contexts.
W—03 Capacity & Training Reciprocal scientific and legal literacy across the network — and training for national and regional prosecutors, investigators, and judicial actors with jurisdiction over environmental crimes, where welcomed. Training rolls out first through the pilots — including, where welcomed, with Ukrainian and Congolese national authorities and with the regional bodies relevant to each.
W—04 Engagement & Case Analysis Convenes the collaborative analysis of possible cases — including an explicit assessment of which forum, national, regional, or international, is best placed to pursue them — and coordinates the flow of products and dialogue with enforcement bodies as individual members choose, under a published code of conduct and transparency register. The case-analysis forum has already convened its first session on the Ukraine pilot (September 2026) and will convene its first DRC session as the pilot's establishment phase completes.

§ 10 — Governance

Federated, lean, accountable

A structure designed to ensure accountability without bureaucratic friction — and to keep every participating community institutionally distinct.

G—01

Steering Committee

Founding academic institutions with a rotating selection of civil society partners; sets strategic direction.

G—02

Coordinating Secretariat

Hosted on a fixed term by an academic institution; handles administration, communications, and logistics.

G—03

Scientific & Technical Advisory Group

Remote-sensing specialists, environmental scientists, and open-source practitioners; guards scientific integrity.

G—04

Legal Advisory Group

International criminal, environmental, and human rights lawyers, with comparative and regional perspectives.

G—05

Civil Society Council

Ensures frontline organisations, Indigenous representatives, and affected-community advocates hold meaningful influence.

G—06

Transparency Register

Public record of funding, membership, methodologies, and significant external engagements; conflicts managed openly.

Pilot case studies are selected by the Steering Committee on the recommendation of the Scientific and Technical Advisory Group, with explicit input from the Civil Society Council — ensuring that frontline and affected-community perspectives are structural to the choice rather than consultative. Each pilot has a convening home among the member organisations, working alongside a Consortium-side coordinator who holds the pilot's shared protocols, timeline, and reporting discipline. Where a pilot is anchored in a pre-existing subconsortium already working actively on the situation — as with the DRC pilot — that subconsortium's internal structures govern the pilot's work, and the Consortium's role is explicitly a supporting one. The Consortium does not organise its members into hierarchies: pilots are convened, not led; members participate, not report. Pilot outputs are peer-reviewed within the Consortium before publication under the usual transparency and conflicts policies, with contributing organisations retaining editorial authority over their own outputs.

The Consortium engages the ICC Office of the Prosecutor — and every other enforcement actor at every level — on the same terms as any external actor. Its value lies in coherence, quality, and breadth of expertise — not in any privileged channel of communication, with any institution.
