Democratic Republic of Congo

Extractive industries, transboundary harm, and community-centred accountability.

P2 — 01 Context

The situation and why it matters

The Democratic Republic of Congo sits at the centre of a web of environmental harms that look very different from those arising in an armed-conflict setting of the Ukrainian kind.

Long-standing extractive-industry activity — cobalt, coltan, copper, gold, timber — has produced severe and in some places irreversible ecological damage, with public-health, livelihood, and cultural consequences for affected communities and Indigenous peoples. The harms are compounded by weak domestic enforcement capacity in some regions, by cross-border ecological spillovers into neighbouring states, and by the deep entanglement of global supply chains in the harm-generating activities.

The DRC also hosts ecosystems of global significance — the Congo Basin forests, in particular — whose destruction has consequences well beyond the national territory. All of this gives the situation a distinctive set of accountability questions, many of which cannot be answered through the frames best suited to armed conflict.

P2 — 02 The question

What the pilot is testing

Can the Consortium's three organising ideas — complementarity, plurality, and the multi-level approach — hold together operationally in a context of long-term extractive harm, where the actors are often corporate rather than military, where transboundary harm is routine rather than exceptional, and where community-centred and Indigenous perspectives must be structural to the method rather than an afterthought?

The pilot treats the DRC as the Consortium's test of geographic and contextual generality.

P2 — 03 The network

What the network is already doing

The DRC pilot is anchored in a Congo-focused subconsortium that is already in place and working actively on the ground. The subconsortium brings the long-standing community relationships, country-specific expertise, and operational presence that any credible accountability work in the DRC requires.

The Consortium's role is complementary to that work: the subconsortium's members carry the ground-level work on the DRC, and the Consortium contributes the connective infrastructure — the family of protocols, the doctrinal and methodological framing of the three organising ideas, the multi-level case-analysis forum, and the links to members whose expertise travels into this context from other situations.

Several Consortium members contribute transferable capacity to the pilot. Remote sensing and spatial analysis, conflict-environment research adapted to extractive-harm contexts, international criminal law and corporate-accountability expertise, international-law and complementarity scholarship, and large-scale human-rights documentation with an active Congo research record all have a place in the pilot's work alongside the subconsortium's own members.

Each engages on its own mandate and through the channels appropriate to it. The Consortium coordinates; it does not represent, and it does not organise its members into any hierarchy. All participants in the pilot operate on equal footing. The subconsortium's members and their outputs are presented in their own terms as the pilot page is populated.

P2 — 04 Enforcement

The enforcement picture

The pilot is designed as a working test of the three organising ideas under extractive-harm and transboundary conditions — complementarity among Congolese authorities, African regional bodies, international mechanisms, and the many non-State actors already engaged; plurality centred on Indigenous and community voices alongside legal and scientific expertise; and the multi-level approach adapted to a context in which the African Union system plays a far more structural role than European instruments.

At the national level, the DRC's own criminal law framework for environmental harm is developing, and the Consortium's protocols are designed to be adapted to and useable within that framework.

At the regional level, the African Union system — including the African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights and the African Commission's extractive-industries and environmental work — provides a significant and under-utilised accountability layer, and one in which Indigenous and community-centred approaches are structurally recognised in a way that is less developed in European instruments. The African Court's capacity to engage environmental harm as a human rights question offers one specific route; regional cooperation on cross-border harms offers another.

At the international level, international criminal mechanisms, the UN human rights system, and emerging business-and-human-rights instruments each matter. The Consortium's pilot work is configured so that evidence and analysis prepared for one layer remains usable at the others.

P2 — 05 Working groups

Working groups and timeline

The DRC pilot's working groups sit within the Congo-focused subconsortium, operating under its own structures. Community-engagement work follows free, prior and informed consent protocols that pre-date the Consortium's own protocol work and that the broader Consortium is learning from.

The methodology-adaptation thread — focused on configuring the shared family of protocols for extractive-harm and transboundary contexts — is a joint undertaking, with the subconsortium's members holding the ground-truth and the broader Consortium bringing the cross-pilot comparability through its work on the Ukraine pilot and its doctrinal workstream.

A regional-and-international engagement thread runs alongside, oriented to the African Union mechanisms, the African Court, UN human rights bodies, and the international layer. The pilot's horizon is set within the subconsortium; the Consortium reports against that horizon rather than imposing one.

P2 — 06 Outputs

Outputs so far

The subconsortium is already producing outputs on the DRC through its own channels. This page will list those outputs as the subconsortium's members choose to feature them here, together with any joint products developed with the broader Consortium.

A short overview of the subconsortium's current workstreams will be added as it is agreed within the subconsortium itself. Joint methodology-adaptation sessions are scheduled for the first quarter of 2027.
Eco-Crim-Consortium

A holistic network of civil society, NGOs, and academia working on the investigation and accountability of large-scale environmental crimes — across international, regional, and national enforcement systems. Institutionally independent of any court or prosecutorial authority. Organised around three ideas: complementarity, plurality, and the multi-level approach.
